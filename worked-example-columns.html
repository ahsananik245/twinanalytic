Worked Example — Part 2

Columns, by Tributary Area

This continues the first worked example — the same 6-storey reinforced concrete frame in Dhaka — into the columns.

Part 1 stopped before column design, on the grounds that it needs the axial and moment pairs a three-dimensional model produces. That is true for the final design. It is not true for preliminary sizing, which is done by tributary area long before a model exists, and which is what this page shows. The limits of the method are set out at the end rather than glossed over.

01The method, and what it cannot do

Tributary area assumes each column carries the floor immediately around it, halfway to the next column in each direction. For a regular frame with roughly equal bays that is close enough to size members and price the frame.

What it does not capture: continuity, which shifts load towards interior columns; the moments a frame attracts under lateral load; and any irregularity. A model gives all three. On a regular building the tributary estimate is usually a little conservative for interior columns and a little unconservative for edge ones, which is why it is a starting point and not an answer.

02Load take-down

From Part 1: dead load 8.50 kN/m², live load 2.00 kN/m². The grid is four bays of 6.0 m by four bays of 4.5 m, giving a 24 × 18 m plan and 25 columns.

ColumnTributary (m²)Dead (kN)Live (kN)Pu (kN)
Interior27.001377.0324.02170.8
Edge13.50688.5162.01085.4
Corner6.75344.281.0542.7

An interior column takes 6.0 × 4.5 = 27.0 m² per floor over six floors, so the dead load is 8.50 × 27.0 × 6 = 1377 kN and the live load 2.00 × 27.0 × 6 = 324 kN. Factored, Pu = 1.2(1377) + 1.6(324) = 2170.8 kN.

Live load reduction has not been applied. BNBC permits a reduction for members carrying large tributary areas over several floors, and taking it would cut the interior column load noticeably. It is left out here because the reduction factor depends on occupancy and on the influence area, and applying it without stating those makes the example harder to check rather than easier. On a real project it would be taken.

A D/C of 0.98 means the section is being worked to within 2 % of its capacity. That is efficient and, for a preliminary size on a load estimate that carries its own uncertainty, too tight. It also turns out not to be the binding problem.

04A slenderness trap worth knowing

Entered with the storey height of 3.0 m, every column in the building comes back slender:

Interior381 mm26.2522.00Slender

That is the wrong answer, and the reason is the input rather than the structure. ACI 318 defines the unsupported length lu as the clear distance between floor members, not the storey height. With 600 mm beams framing into the column, lu is 3.0 − 0.6 = 2.4 m, not 3.0 m.

With lu corrected the same columns are fine. Using the storey height is conservative rather than dangerous — it reports slenderness that is not there — but it sends you into a moment magnification calculation you did not need, and if you respond by growing every column you have made the building heavier and more expensive for nothing.

05Choosing the section

The calculator sizes to the smallest section that carries the load at the steel ratio you ask for, so a higher ratio buys a smaller column. It does not follow that a higher ratio is better:

  • At 3 % the section is 356 mm and slender again — the concrete has been traded away for steel until stability becomes the constraint.
  • At 2 % it is 381 mm with a D/C of 0.98, with nothing left for the load estimate being wrong.
  • At 1 % it is 432 mm, D/C 0.88, kl/r 18.5 — comfortable on both counts, and the least reinforcement to fix and congest.

The 1 % column is the one to take forward, rounded up to a buildable 450 mm square. Rounding up is free: it lowers both the D/C and the slenderness. On a real building the size would then be held constant over two or three floors and stepped, rather than changing every storey, because formwork reuse is worth more than the concrete saved.

Upper floors carry less. The same interior column at level 3 carries four floors rather than six — Pu = 1447 kN, needing 330 mm — and at level 5 just two, Pu = 724 kN, needing 229 mm. That is the argument for stepping the section up the building, and the reason the ground floor column governs the size you detail first.

06Seismic ties — the ACI minimum is not enough

The column calculator returns a tie spacing from the ordinary ACI rules: the least of 16 bar diameters, 48 tie diameters and the column dimension. For a 432 mm column with 25 mm bars and 10 mm ties that is 400 mm.

Part 1 chose a special moment frame, and in a special frame that spacing is nowhere near sufficient near the ends of the column, where the plastic hinge forms. Running the same column through the column tie and hoop spacing calculator:

Confined zone108 mm c/c
Middle zone216 mm c/c
Confinement length Lo457 mm
hx216 mm
Governing rule18.7.5.3(a)
Ties10 mm, 4 legs

Spacing in the confined zone is governed by ACI 318 18.7.5.3(a), a quarter of the least column dimension, giving 432/4 = 108 mm. That is roughly a quarter of the 400 mm the ordinary rules allow, over a length Lo of 457 mm at each end. Detailing a seismic frame to the non-seismic spacing is one of the more consequential mistakes available in reinforced concrete, and it is easy to make, because the first calculator answers the question you asked rather than the one you should have asked.

07Where this stops

Everything above is axial. The columns have been sized for load alone, and a real column in a moment frame carries moment as well — from frame action under gravity, and much more under the seismic case Part 1 showed governs. Those moments need the model, and with them the design moves onto the P-M interaction diagram rather than a pure axial check.

Still outstanding, and still needing a model: biaxial bending, moment magnification where slenderness genuinely applies, storey drift, the strong-column weak-beam check that a special moment frame requires, and the foundations. The calculators for most of those are on this site. They have not been filled in here, because doing so would mean inventing the moments, and a worked example whose numbers cannot be checked is decoration.
